The first thing that stands out in a person born on the day of the Flint is his constant striving for independence and self-reliance. Nature has generously endowed such people with strength of character and an enormous reserve of self-belief. They think in concrete, clear terms, and their habit of always and in everything relying only on their own powers means that they usually know how to do a great deal and often achieve notable success without resorting to anyone's help. Small wonder that among those born on the day of the Flint there are many bright and gifted personalities who have left a deep mark.

To all this the Flint person usually adds no small amount of patience. Once he has set himself a goal, knocking him off course becomes an almost hopeless undertaking: in the end he will get what he is after and will not back away from what he has planned. It is not that obstacles and difficulties do not frighten him at all; he simply tries not to notice them, gets on with his business and builds his life the way he thinks is right.

This manner can cause the Flint plenty of trouble. He often lacks flexibility, and in his words and deeds he can be overly blunt. Diplomacy and flattering courtesy are not merely foreign to him; more often than not, he simply will not accept them. The Flint would sooner keep silent than say something he does not think, though silence costs him an enormous effort: in his view, the truth should be told straight to a person's face, not hidden behind smooth, evasive wording.

That said, it by no means follows that the Flint is invariably harsh and hard in his dealings with others. Rather the opposite: he can come across as a very calm, even-tempered and even polite person. Aggressiveness is simply not in his nature. His rule fits into a short formula: "I don't touch you, so don't touch me." But the moment someone puts pressure on him or so much as raises his voice, a sharp, hard reply follows immediately. In such moments not a trace of the Flint's self-control remains.

Decisiveness is another striking trait of the Flint: few people are able, as he is, to decide things literally on the go. This quickness shows itself in two ways: the Flint may suddenly drop something he has started if he decides it is wrong or pointless, and with the same swiftness throw himself into something new. From the outside this looks like inconsistency, but the real reason is different: the Flint has a vital need to rely on his own convictions and principles in everything, and it is precisely these that determine how he will behave. As for the Flint's chief rule in life, it is as simple as can be: "I decide everything myself."

When making decisions, the Flint is prepared to go even against his own interests if he is convinced that the cause will be better served that way.

It must be acknowledged that the Flint's chief gift is the ability to get what he wants: once he has settled on a goal, this person shows rare persistence and diligence. He is observant, possesses a keen mind and does not recognize insurmountable obstacles. A fine illustration is the Hollywood actor Bruce Lee, born on this day, whom Nature gave neither great height nor outstanding physique. Who could have imagined that a man who looked like a scrawny teenager would become a master of the martial arts, an idol to millions of boys, and prove that strength of character stands above any physical strength!

Another characteristic trait of the Flint is a heightened sense of justice. It usually runs through his whole life like a red thread and finds expression in the most varied forms.

One of the most telling examples is the character of Fyodor Dostoevsky, the great writer and classic of world literature, who was also born on the day of the Flint. His fate was not an easy one: in his youth he was involved in the revolutionary movement, for which he was sentenced to death by firing squad, and the pardon arrived literally a second before the execution. In all of Dostoevsky's books the search for a higher justice remains the central theme; it is no accident that the most famous of his works bears the telling title "Crime and Punishment".

But here is the trouble: what the Flint is convinced of is by no means always the ultimate truth, and this is precisely where his main trap lies. Firmness, resolve, self-belief and a craving for the truth are certainly not negative qualities, but they are like a sharp knife that must be handled with care. Everyone makes mistakes, and the Flint is no exception, and when that happens, his decisiveness and confidence can turn into their opposite. The Flint person is a fighter, and so it matters more for him than for anyone else to choose correctly what exactly he will fight for and by what means.

If, however, the Flint learns to smooth the sharp edges of his character, his undeniable talents and virtues will allow him to rise very high in life. And then there truly will be no insurmountable barriers before him.
